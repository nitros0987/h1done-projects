What the SEC says, and how we align

The rules on AI use

What the SEC says

In relation to the project component of the Leaving Certificate, the SEC Coursework Rules and Procedures 2025/2026, Appendix 2 (pp. 33–36) set the rules for AI. Permitted: background research from credible sources, structuring coursework plans, clarifying research material. Not permitted: generating or paraphrasing content; the candidate's expression must be their own. Every use must be acknowledged in a dedicated appendix: tool and version, developer, date, description of use, prompts where applicable.

How we align

AI never writes report text. The platform only does what Appendix 2 names as permitted: it structures the plan and asks questions. The student's own words become the report. The acknowledgment appendix is generated automatically in the SEC format as the student works, and every answer entry is timestamped. Your job under the Rules is authentication, not detection, and authentication needs a trail, not a hunch: what was written at each stage, and what AI was used for, exportable in one click.

What the SEC says, and how we align

Feedback: individual or for the group?

This is the question we hear most, so here is the wording and exactly how we read it.

What the guidelines say (Role of the teacher, pp. 12–13)

The teacher "may support students by… offering prompt questions… to stimulate and support students' planning and critical thinking throughout the process", and "it is important that each student takes an individualised approach to the work". On feedback:

"The feedback provided to students should be general and nondirective in nature. Teachers should not provide any excessive or inappropriate support to students, such as editing draft work or providing model text or answers to be used in the students' evidence of learning."

Applied Geography Project guidelines (NCCA, Dec 2025). The Biology in Practice Investigation guidelines (Nov 2024) carry the same section, p. 12.

How we read it, and how we align

The line binds what feedback you may give: general and nondirective, never model text, never answers, never draft-editing. It does not forbid individualised questions: prompt questions are a named support, and an individualised approach is expected. So the platform holds that exact line: individualised prompt questions for every student, on their own project (that is the teaching), never model text or answers (that is the hard wall). Teacher feedback stays yours and stays general: an AI-drafted suggestion only ever appears when you click to request one, you preview it before anything is sent, and nothing reaches a student without your approval. If the SEC clarifies or amends any of this, the compliance documentation changes before the features do. We adapt to the Rules, always.

The gap

Every project starts the same. None stays the same.

All thirty students open the same brief on the same day. From there, each project branches: one student's research takes them to a source the others never touch, one hits a dead end, one is three sub-questions behind where the plan said they'd be. By the draft stage you are not supervising one assignment. You are supervising thirty projects that share a title and nothing else.

That is what makes the new LC project the hard job for the teacher: the deeper it goes, the more unique each student's route becomes, and the small sub-stages along the way are exactly what a generic assignment flow cannot see or hold. And it is specific all the way down: this exam (the Leaving Cert), this subject, this year's brief, this student, their sub-stages. Nothing off-the-shelf accounts for all of that, because it is different for every single student in the room.

If you want to start tomorrow

The honest free alternatives

This is how it is done today in Workspace: one assignment per section, students submit, and the teacher opens each student's work, corrects it individually and leaves feedback. It works. Here is what each piece costs you:

  • Classroom, staged Workspace: one assignment per stage, each with its own deadline. Free, works today. No gating between stages, no export, no AI-use record, and every student gets the same stages regardless of where their project actually went.
  • Google Forms checkpoints Workspace: a short form at each stage creates a timestamped audit trail. Free. You assemble it per class, per year, and read the evidence out of a spreadsheet.
  • Docs as a tracker Workspace: a template doc per student; revision history is the timestamp evidence (and it is the evidence you already have). Manual to review, and it shows edits, not answers.

All three are worth doing. They cost hours of assembly each year, none of them produce a verbatim per-stage export or an AI-use record, and none of them branch with the student.

The alternative to assembling it yourself

How H1Done Projects runs a project

Research→ Plan→ Draft→ Revision→ Write-up
  • The student always knows the next move. The platform asks the right question for their stage, they answer in their own words, and their answers become the report. AI never writes report text; it structures the plan and asks the questions, which are named permitted uses in the SEC rules.
  • The sub-stages are built for that student. Not a generic template: the questions come from the LC spec, this year's official brief, the subject, and the answers that student has already given. As their project branches, their stages branch with it.
  • You see each point without chasing it. Every stage has a deadline you set, every answer is timestamped on entry, and the whole route is viewable at any time, per student or as a class board.
  • Feedback stays yours. General and nondirective, as the guidelines require. An AI-drafted suggestion appears only when you click to request one, you preview it before it sends, and nothing reaches a student without your approval.
  • The export does the paperwork. One click: the student's verbatim answers under the SEC headings, plus the AI-use acknowledgment appendix in the SEC format.

That is the missing layer described above, built: the branching process taught, the evidence trail kept, the teacher's supervising job made visible instead of guessable.
